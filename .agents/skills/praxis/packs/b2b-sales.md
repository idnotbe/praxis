# B2B sales pack

Version: 0.1.0. Status: illustrative, review-required. Owner: deployment operator.
These are Praxis-designed examples and proposed heuristics, not validated sales
research or any company's pricing policy. Replace them with approved domain
sources before relying on them as organization-specific guidance.

## Scope and decision model

Support discovery preparation, price-objection responses, and sales-to-delivery
handoffs. Produce a usable agenda, response draft, or handoff with open decisions.
Do not invent product claims, customer facts, legal terms, or approval authority.

Identify the business problem, consequences, buying criteria, stakeholders,
budget context, alternatives, decision process, timing, and evidence. Separate
an internal champion's enthusiasm from evidence of an authorized buying decision.
Ask only questions that would change the recommendation; unknowns need not block
a clearly labeled draft.

## Mandatory constraints

Load applicable pricing, disclosure, product-claim, and commitment constraints
from authorized sources. None of their thresholds is supplied by this pack.
Record unavailable policy evidence as UNKNOWN; do not fabricate a discount cap.
An unapproved commercial term can be explored internally but not represented as
an authorized offer. Follow the common skill's approval and action contract.

## S-01: Diagnose price resistance before choosing a concession

Applicability: a prospect says the price is too high. It may reflect budget,
uncertain value, competitor comparison, procurement tactics, or scope mismatch.

Strategy: identify the comparison and decision criterion; connect value to the
customer's stated problem; compare scope, sequencing, and terms where authorized;
then define the decision owner and next step. A discount is one possible option,
not the automatic answer. Switch strategy when evidence identifies a hard budget
constraint or a materially different alternative. Do not diagnose motive as fact.

Bad: "We can definitely cut the price by 20% and deliver next week."
Better: "Which scope and outcome are you comparing? We can assess a smaller first
phase; any price or delivery commitment needs the applicable approval."
Near-miss: a fully approved 20% offer is not invalid simply because it is a discount.

Detection: find asserted price, scope, and dates; retrieve approval evidence.
Judgment: determine whether the reply addresses the actual objection without
inventing a guarantee. A discount keyword alone is not a violation.

## S-02: Make the next decision concrete

Applicability: discovery or a follow-up ends without a clear next step.
Strategy: name the pending customer decision, required evidence, owner, and
proposed date. If the buyer needs internal review, provide a shareable evidence
summary rather than demanding another meeting without a purpose.

Bad: "We will stay aligned and keep momentum."
Better: "The operations lead will review the two scope options; the sponsor will
confirm which option should enter procurement. The review date is not agreed."
Near-miss: leaving a date explicitly unconfirmed is better than inventing one.

Check whether the next step is useful and feasible, not just whether four fields
are filled. Do not automatically create a CRM task or send the message.

## S-03: Transfer commitments, not optimism

For a sales-to-delivery handoff, separate agreed scope, assumptions, exclusions,
acceptance conditions, stakeholders, unresolved promises, dependencies, and
source documents. A signed term and a salesperson's expectation are different.
Escalate conflicting commitments; do not silently choose the easiest version.

## Completion and learning

A useful result responds to the specific buyer issue, exposes unsupported
commitments, and offers an actionable next decision. Check quotations: a
customer saying "guarantee Friday" is not the seller making that promise.
After an observed outcome, retain a scoped case only with authorized storage.
A won deal does not prove that one negotiation tactic caused the outcome.
